# Competitors and similar products

Survey for turning the Markerless AR-to-VR Portal prototype into a product. All pages were opened on
**21 November 2026**. Prices are the published ones on that day and exclude tax unless stated.

How to read the tables:

- A fact without a remark was read on the linked page.
- **(snippet)** means it was seen only in a search-engine summary, because the page blocked automated reading.
- **not published** means the vendor shows no price. **not verified** means no page confirming it was found.
- Store ratings are from the US or UK App Store. Google Play pages could not be read by the tools used, so
  Android ratings are missing throughout.

Our product, for comparison: an Android app (ARCore) and a self-hosted server. The user scans a room once, the
server builds a visual map and a room plan and finds the real door opening, and in later sessions a virtual
door stands in that opening without markers. The user walks through it into a virtual space and back.

Apple and Meta offer free building blocks that only work on their own devices: ARKit can save and restore a
room map on an iPhone, Apple's location anchors
[work outdoors only](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/argeotrackingconfiguration.md), and
Meta's [shared spatial anchors](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/unity-shared-spatial-anchors/)
work on Quest headsets within one room. Android has no built-in equivalent, and ARCore's
[Scene Semantics is outdoor-only](https://developers.google.com/ar/develop/scene-semantics), so it cannot find
indoor walls or doors.

## 2. What each group shows

### AR portals

- Every portal app found places the door by hand on the floor in each session. None finds the same physical
  doorway again. The one example of a portal tied to a real entrance is a Niantic trade-show demo.
- The genre lost three hosts in two years: Meta Spark, Adobe Aero and 8th Wall. Each time creators lost their
  work. Adobe deleted user data two weeks after the app stopped.
- Portal apps are mostly on iOS. A Google Play search for "AR portal" returned one small app (6 reviews).
- First run in the better ones is three steps: scan the ground, tap to place, walk through, plus a visible
  "reset" button (Portals AR).
- Complaints: too little content, purchases that lost their value after a redesign (Portals AR), placed
  objects that drift when the phone moves ([ARLOOPA](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/arloopa-scan-discover-ar/id935637249), 4.7/5 from 1.4K ratings).
- Technique worth copying from Snap's Portal asset: the virtual world sits inside an "occluder" shell with one
  opening, and crossing the threshold fires enter and exit events that can drive sound and light.

### Museums, heritage, tourism

- Venues pay yearly for a platform (Smartify, Artivive) or commission a project (Histovery, Belvedere,
  St Albans). Visitors pay per place or story, between EUR 0.99 and EUR 19.
- The Belvedere found GPS and beacons too imprecise indoors and paid for a pre-scan with visual positioning.
  That is the same idea as ours, bought from a platform whose web hosting is closing.
- Paid AR that fails on site is punished: Vivid Walks has 2.9/5, with reviews saying it did not work at the
  Colosseum after purchase.
- Small heritage apps go stale: Hidden Florence 3D (2022), KaviAR (2022), Slano AR (2019). Croatian examples
  found are one-off, image-triggered projects.
- Histovery hands visitors a venue tablet. That removes installation and the question of which phone the
  visitor owns.
- Histovery and RARO advertise that historians or archaeologists checked the content.

### Interiors, property, furniture

- Property tools show a scan or a model on a screen. None found ties a walk-through to the real door of a
  show unit. Trimble's professional tool still needs QR markers indoors.
- Furniture apps have set the conventions people already know: a short "move your phone" prompt, a visible
  surface indicator, and an explicit step into AR (Google's viewer shows 3D first, then "View in your space").
- IKEA Kreativ processes the scan elsewhere and sends an e-mail when the room is ready. Planner 5D states the
  wait (3–10 minutes) and asks the user to keep the app open.

### Room scanners

- Room-plan scanning is in practice an iPhone-with-LiDAR feature. magicplan says Android is not supported for
  scanning. Polycam's room capture is iOS-only, with an Android version "coming soon". IKEA's full scan,
  Houzz Pro and Planner 5D need LiDAR.
- Apple's RoomPlan is the reference design: live outlines on detected walls and openings, one short
  instruction at a time from a fixed set (move closer, move away, slow down, turn on a light, low texture),
  and a small model of the room to approve at the end
  ([RoomPlan documentation](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/roomplan/roomcapturesession/instruction.json)).
- Complaints: redesigns that add steps (Matterport), lost scans (Matterport, Scaniverse), free quotas used up
  by test projects (magicplan), walls missed and crashes on large rooms (AR Plan 3D).

### Platforms that keep content in place

- Standard plans keep the customer's imagery or maps in the vendor's cloud. Running on the customer's own
  hardware is an Enterprise option with no published price at Niantic, MultiSet, ReactVision and Immersal.
- Usage is metered: about USD 0.005–0.01 per position fix (Niantic, MultiSet), USD 0.60 per server minute
  (ReactVision), or a cap on requests per second (EasyAR).
- They return a position. Where the content goes is left to the developer. None of the pages read mentions
  finding door openings.
- They are developer kits. A venue cannot use one without someone building an app on top.
- ReactVision sells positioning inside the same library our app uses, from USD 25 a month. For anyone
  building on ViroReact it is the obvious ready-made choice.
- Accuracy figures are vendor claims. No independent comparison was found.

## 3. Guidance from Apple and Google on AR screens

From Apple's [AR guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/augmented-reality)
and Google's ARCore design pages
([onboarding](https://developers.google.com/ar/design/interaction/ux),
[interface](https://developers.google.com/ar/design/interaction/ui),
[environment](https://developers.google.com/ar/design/environment/definition)):

- Teach in context with a picture or animation, not with a long tutorial up front.
- Ask for the camera when it is needed and say why.
- Keep on-screen controls and text to a minimum; put essential text on the screen, not in the 3D scene.
- Name problems in plain words (too dark, moving too fast, plain surface) and say how to fix them. Do not
  blame the user. Always offer a reset.
- While finding the place again, hide unrelated controls, and consider hiding the content until it is back in
  position.
- Do not ask for fast or large movements. Tell people how much space they need and to mind their surroundings.
- Let the user choose the moment of entering AR, and mark the change with a fade.

## 4. Conclusions

### Adopt

1. **A guided scan with live paint on the walls and one instruction at a time**, ending with a plan to
   approve ("is this your doorway?"). This is what RoomPlan apps do on iPhones, and nobody offers it on
   Android.
2. **An honest wait.** Named steps, a time estimate, permission to leave the app, and a notification when the
   room is ready.
3. **Three-step first run and a visible way to reset** the door.
4. **A hidden door until its place is confirmed**, with advice that gets more specific as attempts fail.
5. **Venue-loaned devices and a visitor mode**, as Histovery does. It also sidesteps our Android-only limit.
6. **Published per-venue pricing.** The visible range for venue software is roughly EUR 1,000–10,000 a year
   (Artivive, Smartify), with content production sold separately.
7. **Threshold effects**: sound and a light change when the user crosses the doorway.
8. **Named sources for the content.** For a historic space, say who checked it and credit the model.

### Avoid

1. Depending on someone else's hosted AR platform for the core function.
2. Charging before the door has appeared on site, and any flow where a failed scan or test costs the user.
3. A door that slides out of the doorway. Show honestly when the position is unconfirmed and re-fit it.
4. Long text tutorials, and a screen crowded with controls over the camera.
5. Silent failure in dim rooms or on plain walls, glass and mirrors.
6. Losing a user's rooms on an update. Rooms need a backup or export path.

### Where our approach is different

- **The door finds its real doorway by itself, every session.** No surveyed portal app does this, and no
  surveyed platform advertises detecting door openings.
- **The map stays on the customer's own machine.** Elsewhere that is a custom Enterprise contract.
- **No charge per position fix.** A local server has almost no running cost per use, which allows a flat
  price per venue.
- **Android, without LiDAR and without printed codes.** Room-plan scanning on Android is a gap in the market.
- **It does not depend on a platform that can be switched off.**

### Where it is weaker

- One client: an Android app on React Native. Competing platforms ship kits for five or more targets.
- Someone has to run a PC with the server on the venue's network. The setup today is for a developer.
- Proven for one room on public test data. A map from a real phone recording has not been built yet.
- No measured accuracy to set against the vendors' claims until the project's own test protocol is run.
- ReactVision can offer much of this to the same developers for USD 25 a month, with the data in its cloud.
